## Settling in

Let yourself be still now.

Feel the pillow under your head and the weight of the blanket over you. Breathe in slowly. And let the breath go, all the way out.

[pause]

Tonight's story is a short one, and a kind one. It has no battles and no kings. It is about two widows on a dusty road, a field of barley at harvest time, and the quiet ways God takes care of people who think they have lost everything.

You don't need to follow every detail. Just let it unfold.

[pause]

## Part one: a family in Moab

The story begins in the days when the judges ruled Israel, long before there were any kings. There was a famine in the land. And a man from Bethlehem in Judah took his wife and his two sons, and went to live in the land of Moab, on the far side of the Dead Sea, where there was food.

The man's name was Elimelech. His wife's name was Naomi. Their sons were Mahlon and Chilion.

They settled in Moab. And there, far from home, Elimelech died, and Naomi was left with her two sons.

The sons married women of Moab. One was named Orpah, and the other was named Ruth. For about ten years they lived there together.

And then both of Naomi's sons died too.

[pause]

Naomi was left without her husband, and without her children, in a country that was not her own. Three widows in one house, and no one to provide for them.

[pause]

## Part two: the road back

Then news came from home. Naomi heard that the LORD had come to the help of His people, and that there was bread in Bethlehem again. So she decided to go back. Her two daughters-in-law set out on the road with her.

But somewhere along the way, Naomi stopped and turned to them. She told them to go back, each of them to her mother's home. And she blessed them:

> May the LORD show you loving devotion, as you have shown to your dead and to me. [Ruth 1:8]

She kissed them, and they wept aloud. They said they would go with her to her people. But Naomi told them she had nothing to give them. She had no more sons to become their husbands. Her life, she said, was more bitter than theirs, because the hand of the LORD had gone out against her.

They wept again. And Orpah kissed her mother-in-law goodbye, and went home.

But Ruth clung to her.

[pause]

Naomi tried once more. Look, she said, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her gods. Go with her.

And Ruth answered with some of the most loved words in the whole Bible:

> Do not urge me to leave you or to turn from following you. For wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. [Ruth 1:16]

> Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. [Ruth 1:17]

When Naomi saw that Ruth's mind was made up, she said nothing more. And the two women walked on together.

[long pause]

## Part three: call me Mara

They came to Bethlehem, and the whole town was stirred. The women said to one another, “Can this be Naomi?” [Ruth 1:19]

Naomi's name means pleasant. And she answered them:

> Do not call me Naomi… Call me Mara, because the Almighty has dealt quite bitterly with me. [Ruth 1:20]

> I went away full, but the LORD has brought me back empty. [Ruth 1:21]

[pause]

It is a very honest thing to say. Naomi doesn't pretend. She says out loud that she is grieving, that she feels empty, that she doesn't understand what God is doing. The Bible keeps her words, and it doesn't scold her for them.

But notice what she doesn't see yet. She says she came back empty. Walking beside her is Ruth, who has given up everything to stay with her. And the story adds one small detail, almost in passing. They arrived in Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest.

[pause]

## Part four: the field of Boaz

The two women needed food. In Israel, the law told farmers not to harvest right to the edges of their fields, and not to go back for grain they had dropped. What was left was for the poor and for foreigners to gather. It was called gleaning.

Ruth was both poor and a foreigner. So she said to Naomi, let me go out to the fields and gather grain behind someone who will be kind to me. And Naomi said, “Go ahead, my daughter.” [Ruth 2:2]

So Ruth went out. And the Bible says she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to a man named Boaz, who was from Elimelech's family.

She happened to. The story says it lightly, but it wants us to smile.

[pause]

Just then, Boaz himself arrived from Bethlehem. He greeted his workers, “The LORD be with you.” [Ruth 2:4] And they answered, “The LORD bless you.” [Ruth 2:4]

He noticed the young woman gleaning, and asked whose she was. The foreman told him she was the Moabite woman who had come back with Naomi, and that she had been working since morning, with only a short rest in the shelter.

Boaz went over to her. He told her to stay in his field, close to his servant girls. He told her that when she was thirsty, she could drink from the water jars his men had filled.

Ruth bowed down to the ground. She asked him why he was being so kind to her, a foreigner.

And Boaz answered that he had heard everything: how she had cared for her mother-in-law since her husband died, how she had left her father and mother and the land of her birth, to come to a people she had never known. And he blessed her:

> May the LORD repay your work, and may you receive a rich reward from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have taken refuge. [Ruth 2:12]

[pause]

Under His wings. Picture a bird gathering its young close at night. That is where Ruth had come, without knowing it, when she said, your God will be my God.

[pause]

At mealtime Boaz called her over to share the bread, and handed her roasted grain, and she ate until she was satisfied, with some left over. When she went back to work, he quietly told his young men to let her gather even among the sheaves, and to pull out some stalks on purpose and leave them for her to find.

Ruth gleaned until evening. When she beat out the grain, it came to about an ephah of barley, enough to fill a large basket.

[pause]

## Part five: Naomi's hope

Ruth carried it home. Naomi saw how much she had gathered, and Ruth gave her the food she had saved from her own meal.

“Where did you glean today?” [Ruth 2:19] Naomi asked. “Blessed be the man who noticed you.” [Ruth 2:19]

And Ruth told her. The man's name was Boaz.

Something changed in Naomi then. For the first time in our story, she speaks of God's kindness:

> May he be blessed by the LORD, who has not withdrawn His kindness from the living or the dead. [Ruth 2:20]

The man, she said, is a close relative. One of our kinsman-redeemers.

[pause]

A kinsman-redeemer was a close relative with a special duty. If someone in the family fell on hard times and had to sell their land, the redeemer had the right to buy it back, so that it stayed in the family. He was someone who could step in and rescue.

So Ruth stayed in the fields of Boaz, all through the barley harvest and the wheat harvest after it. And every evening she came home to Naomi.

[long pause]

## Part six: the threshing floor

When the harvest was ending, Naomi said to Ruth, “My daughter, should I not seek a resting place for you, that it may be well with you?” [Ruth 3:1]

Boaz, she said, would be winnowing barley that night at the threshing floor, separating the grain from the chaff in the evening breeze. She told Ruth to wash, and put on perfume and her best clothes, and to go there. When Boaz had eaten and lain down to sleep beside the heap of grain, Ruth was to go quietly, uncover his feet, and lie down there.

It was a bold plan. It was a way of asking, without words, whether he would take her under his care.

Ruth did everything Naomi said.

[pause]

At midnight, Boaz woke with a start, and turned over, and there was a woman lying at his feet.

“Who are you?” [Ruth 3:9] he asked.

And she said:

> I am your servant Ruth… Spread the corner of your garment over me, for you are a kinsman-redeemer. [Ruth 3:9]

The corner of your garment. In Hebrew it is the same word as wings. Boaz had prayed that Ruth would find refuge under God's wings. Now she was asking Boaz to be part of the answer to his own prayer.

[pause]

Boaz was moved. He blessed her, and told her not to be afraid. The whole town, he said, knew she was a woman of noble character, and he would do what she asked. But there was one difficulty. There was another relative, closer than he was, who had the first right to redeem. If that man would, then good. If not, Boaz promised, as surely as the LORD lives, he would do it himself.

Ruth stayed until just before dawn, and slipped away before anyone could recognize her. Boaz poured six measures of barley into her shawl, so she would not go back to her mother-in-law empty-handed.

When she told Naomi everything, Naomi said, “Wait, my daughter,” [Ruth 3:18] “until you find out how things go, for he will not rest unless he has resolved the matter today.” [Ruth 3:18]

[pause]

## Part seven: at the town gate

That same morning, Boaz went up to the town gate, where business was done in front of witnesses, and sat down. Soon the other relative came by, and Boaz called him over. Then he gathered ten of the elders of the town.

He explained that Naomi had land to sell, the land that had belonged to Elimelech. The closer relative had the first right to buy it.

“I will redeem it,” [Ruth 4:4] the man said.

Then Boaz told him the rest. Whoever bought the land would also take Ruth, Mahlon's widow, as his wife, so that the family name would not be lost.

And the man changed his mind. He said that he could not do it without putting his own inheritance at risk. He told Boaz to take the right of redemption himself. And in the custom of those days, to seal the agreement, he took off his sandal and gave it to Boaz.

[pause]

Boaz turned to the elders and all the people at the gate. You are witnesses today, he said, that I am buying all that belonged to Elimelech and his sons, and that Ruth will be my wife.

And they answered, “We are witnesses.” [Ruth 4:11]

They blessed the marriage. They prayed that Ruth would be like Rachel and Leah, the mothers of Israel, and that Boaz's house would be famous in Bethlehem.

[long pause]

## Part eight: a son for Naomi

So Boaz married Ruth. The LORD gave her a child, and she had a son.

And the story turns, at the very end, back to Naomi. The women of Bethlehem, the same women who had seen her come home empty, gathered round her and said:

> Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a kinsman-redeemer. [Ruth 4:14]

> He will renew your life and sustain you in your old age. For your daughter-in-law, who loves you and is better to you than seven sons, has given him birth. [Ruth 4:15]

Naomi took the child and laid him on her lap, and cared for him. The neighbor women said, “A son has been born to Naomi,” [Ruth 4:17] and they named him Obed.

[pause]

She went away full, and came back empty. And now, with the baby on her lap and Ruth beside her, she is full again.

[pause]

Obed grew up and became the father of Jesse. And Jesse became the father of David, the shepherd boy who would one day be king of Israel.

[pause]

## What Scripture remembers

Hundreds of years later, when Matthew wrote down the family line of Jesus, he included Ruth by name: a widow from Moab who once gathered leftover grain at the edge of a field.

> Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, Obed the father of Jesse [Matthew 1:5]

[pause]

## Closing

God is hardly mentioned in this story. There are no visions here, no voices from heaven, no miracles. Just a famine ending, a woman who happened to choose the right field, a kind man, and an old law about leaving grain at the edges.

And yet, from the first chapter to the last, God is quietly at work in all of it. Through ordinary days. Through ordinary kindness.

So if tonight your life feels more like Naomi's on the road home, empty and tired, remember how her story ended. She could not see what God was doing. But He had not withdrawn His kindness, not from her and not from Ruth.

[long pause]

Let your breathing slow now.

Picture the barley fields outside Bethlehem in the evening, golden and still. The harvest gathered in. The workers gone home. The last of the light on the hills.

[pause]

You can rest, the way Ruth rested, under His wings.

Rest now.

Goodnight.

[long pause]
