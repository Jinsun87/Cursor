## Settling in

Close your eyes, if they aren't closed already.

Let your shoulders drop, a little, away from your ears. Let the bed hold you.

Breathe in slowly, as far as is comfortable. And let it go, all the way out, without hurrying.

Once more. In. And out.

Notice the weight of your hands, wherever they are resting. There is nothing you need to do with them now. The day is finished. Whatever was left undone can wait until morning.

[pause]

Tonight's story is very old. It begins about four thousand years ago, among people who travelled with their flocks and slept in tents, under a sky with no city lights to dim it. It is the story of a woman named Sarah.

You don't need to remember any of it. Let it pass by, the way clouds pass a window, while you settle further into the quiet.

[pause]

Let's begin.

## Part one: leaving home

Her name, when our story begins, was Sarai. The name Sarah came later, and it came as a gift. We'll get there.

Sarai lived in Ur of the Chaldeans, an old city near the great rivers, and she was married to a man named Abram. The Bible tells us one thing about her life there before anything else. It says it plainly, in a single line:

> But Sarai was barren; she had no children. [Genesis 11:30]

[pause]

One day Abram's father, Terah, set out from Ur with his family. He took Abram and Sarai, and his grandson Lot, whose own father had died young. They meant to go all the way to the land of Canaan. But when they reached a town called Haran, they stopped, and they stayed. The years went by in Haran, and Terah grew old and died there.

It was in Haran that the LORD spoke to Abram, and what He said changed everything.

> Leave your country, your kindred, and your father's household, and go to the land I will show you. [Genesis 12:1]

There was no map. There was no address. There was a promise: that God would make Abram into a great nation, and that through him all the families of the earth would be blessed.

Abram was seventy-five years old. Sarai was about ten years younger. Picture the evening they decided. The lamps in the tents. The animals settling for the night. The long road south and west, waiting in the dark beyond the town.

They packed the tents. They gathered the flocks and the people of their household. And they went.

[pause]

We are not told what Sarai felt. Perhaps she was afraid. Perhaps she was ready for something new. What we are told is simply that she went, mile after mile, toward a land she had never seen.

Most courage looks like that. Not a battle. Just the next step, taken in trust, and then the one after it.

[pause]

## Part two: Egypt

They came into Canaan and travelled through it slowly. At a great tree near Shechem, the Oak of Moreh, the LORD appeared to Abram and promised this land to his family. Abram built an altar there. Then they moved on, into the hill country near Bethel, and pitched their tents again, and Abram built another altar and called on the name of the LORD.

Then the rain failed. A famine came, the kind that dries the wells and empties the fields, and it was severe. So the whole household went south, down into Egypt, to find food.

Here the story turns uncomfortable, and it is best told honestly.

As they came near Egypt, Abram said to Sarai that she was a beautiful woman, and that the Egyptians might kill him to take her. So he asked her to say that she was his sister. It was half true. Much later we learn that she was his half-sister, the daughter of his father. But it hid the truth that mattered most, and it left her unprotected.

Pharaoh's officials saw her, praised her to Pharaoh, and she was taken into the palace.

[pause]

You can imagine the nights she spent there, in a place she had never asked to enter, far from her tent, far from anyone who should have protected her.

But she was not forgotten. The LORD sent severe plagues on Pharaoh and his household because of Sarai. And Pharaoh, when he learned the truth, called Abram in and said:

> What have you done to me? Why didn't you tell me she was your wife? [Genesis 12:18]

He gave Sarai back, and sent them on their way with everything they had.

[pause]

If you have ever been let down by the very people who should have looked after you, this part of Sarai's story belongs to you too. She did nothing wrong here. And God did not leave her to carry it alone.

[pause]

## Part three: the long wait

They came back into Canaan, and the years began to pass. Abram became very wealthy, in flocks and herds, in silver and gold. The household grew. There were more servants, more tents, more animals at the wells.

But one thing did not change. There was still no child.

One night the LORD took Abram outside, under that wide, dark sky, and said to him:

> Now look to the heavens and count the stars, if you are able. [Genesis 15:5]

And then:

> So shall your offspring be. [Genesis 15:5]

The Bible says Abram believed the LORD. But believing is one thing, and waiting is another. Ten years went by in Canaan. Sarai grew older. The promise must have felt, some nights, like something meant for somebody else.

[pause]

At last Sarai made a decision of her own. She had an Egyptian servant named Hagar, and she said to Abram:

> Look now, the LORD has prevented me from bearing children. Please go to my maidservant; perhaps I can build a family by her. [Genesis 16:2]

It was a custom of those times. But it did not bring the peace Sarai hoped for. When Hagar found she was expecting a child, she began to look down on her mistress. Sarai, hurt and angry, blamed Abram, and then she treated Hagar so harshly that Hagar ran away into the desert.

This is the hardest part of Sarai's story, and the Bible does not hide it. Waiting had worn her down, and the hurt she felt spilled out onto someone with even less power than she had.

[pause]

But God did not forget Hagar either. The angel of the LORD found her beside a spring in the desert, on the road to Shur. He spoke to her kindly, told her to go back, and promised that her son would have descendants too many to count. She was to call him Ishmael, which means God hears, because the LORD had heard her distress.

And Hagar, alone by that spring, gave God a name of her own:

> You are the God who sees me [Genesis 16:13]

She went back. Her son was born, and Abram named him Ishmael. Abram was eighty-six years old.

[pause]

If tonight you are tired of waiting, or sorry for something you did while you waited, you are in good company. The God who saw Hagar by the spring sees you too.

[long pause]

## Part four: new names

Thirteen more years went by. Abram was ninety-nine years old when the LORD appeared to him again, and said, “I am God Almighty.” [Genesis 17:1]

This time, God gave them both new names. Abram, which means something like exalted father, would now be Abraham, because God said:

> I have made you a father of many nations. [Genesis 17:5]

And then God spoke about Sarai. She was not to be called Sarai any more, but Sarah. Both names mean princess, and the new one carried a promise with it. God said:

> I will bless her, and she will be the mother of nations; kings of peoples will descend from her. [Genesis 17:16]

So that is where the words mother of nations come from. Not from the meaning of her name, but from God's promise about her.

Abraham fell on his face, and he laughed. He said to himself, could a child be born to a man a hundred years old? Could Sarah give birth at ninety?

Then Abraham thought of the son he already had, the boy Ishmael, now thirteen years old. And he said to God:

> O that Ishmael might live under Your blessing! [Genesis 17:18]

God heard him. He promised to bless Ishmael too, and to make him a great nation. But the promise He had made all those years ago was about something more. God said that Sarah herself would bear a son. Not a servant. Not someone in her place. Sarah. And God gave the child his name before he was even born. He was to be called Isaac. And He told Abraham when: at this time next year.

[pause]

Hold on to that word for a moment: impossible. Most of us know what it is to set a hope down because it seemed too late. Sarah's story doesn't hurry past that feeling. It lets it sit, honestly, before anything changes.

[pause]

## Part five: visitors at Mamre

Not long after, in the heat of the day, Abraham was sitting at the entrance of his tent by the great trees of Mamre. He looked up and saw three men standing nearby.

He ran to meet them, bowed low, and begged them to rest in the shade. Water was brought to wash their feet. Abraham hurried into the tent and asked Sarah to bake bread, quickly, from the finest flour. A young calf was prepared, with curds and milk, and the visitors ate under the tree while Abraham stood beside them.

Then they asked, “Where is your wife Sarah?” [Genesis 18:9]

“There, in the tent,” [Genesis 18:9] he said.

And the LORD said that He would return at that time the next year, and that Sarah would have a son.

[pause]

Sarah was just behind him, listening at the entrance to the tent. She was old now, long past the age of having children. And when she heard those words, she laughed quietly to herself. She said:

> After I am worn out and my master is old, will I now have this pleasure? [Genesis 18:12]

The LORD heard. He asked Abraham why Sarah had laughed. And then He asked the question that has been remembered ever since:

> Is anything too difficult for the LORD? [Genesis 18:14]

Sarah was afraid, and she said, “I did not laugh.” [Genesis 18:15]

And the LORD answered, gently and simply, “No… but you did laugh.” [Genesis 18:15]

[pause]

There is no anger in this. Sarah isn't punished for laughing. The promise simply stands, steady and unchanged, whether she can believe it yet or not.

[pause]

## Part six: Gerar

There is one more hard moment before the joy, and it sounds familiar. The household moved south again, to a place called Gerar. And once more, Abraham said of Sarah, “She is my sister.” [Genesis 20:2] The king of Gerar, Abimelech, had her brought to his house.

But God came to Abimelech in a dream one night and warned him that she was a married woman. Abimelech gave her back to Abraham, with gifts, and said to Sarah that she was completely cleared, so that no one could think badly of her.

Twice now, Sarah had been put in danger by someone else's fear. And twice, God had brought her safely home.

[pause]

## Part seven: laughter

And then it happened, quietly, at exactly the time God had said.

> Now the LORD attended to Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah what He had promised. [Genesis 21:1]

Sarah, who had waited so long, gave birth to a son. Abraham was a hundred years old. And Abraham gave the boy the name God had chosen, Isaac, a name that means he laughs.

Listen to what Sarah said, holding him:

> God has made me laugh, and everyone who hears of this will laugh with me. [Genesis 21:6]

And she added:

> Who would have told Abraham that Sarah would nurse children? Yet I have borne him a son in his old age. [Genesis 21:7]

[pause]

Think about that. The same woman who once laughed behind a tent door because the promise seemed impossible, now laughed for joy with the promise in her arms. The same laughter, years apart, meaning two entirely different things.

[long pause]

## Part eight: a hard goodbye

The child grew, and on the day Isaac was weaned, Abraham held a great feast. But Sarah saw Ishmael, Hagar's son, mocking her little boy. And the old pain came back.

She asked Abraham to send Hagar and her son away, so that Ishmael would not share Isaac's inheritance. Abraham was deeply distressed. Ishmael was his son too, and he loved him.

But God told Abraham not to be distressed. Through Isaac the promise would continue, and God would make a nation of Ishmael as well.

Early the next morning, Abraham gave Hagar bread and a skin of water and sent her away with the boy. They wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba until the water ran out.

And once again, the God who sees was there. God heard the voice of the boy. The angel of God called to Hagar and told her not to be afraid. Then God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. She filled the skin and gave her son a drink. And God was with the boy as he grew up.

[pause]

It is a part of the story that doesn't tie up neatly. Families are complicated. Even families who live by great promises carry ordinary grief. But no one in this story falls outside God's care, not Sarah, and not Hagar, and not the boy under the bush in the desert.

[pause]

## Part nine: a place to rest

Sarah lived to see her son grow into a man. She lived to be a hundred and twenty-seven years old. And she died at Hebron, in the land of Canaan, and Abraham went in to mourn for her and to weep.

Then Abraham, who had owned no land in all his years of wandering, went to the people of that place and said:

> I am a foreigner and an outsider among you. Give me a burial site among you so that I can bury my dead. [Genesis 23:4]

The people of the land answered him kindly. They called him God's chosen one among them, and told him to bury his dead in the finest of their tombs.

Abraham rose and bowed to them. He asked them to speak for him to a man named Ephron, who owned a cave at the end of his field, the cave of Machpelah, near Mamre. Abraham wanted to buy it, at its full price.

Ephron was sitting there among his people, at the gate of the town. And he offered it as a gift. “I give you the field,” [Genesis 23:11] he said, “and I give you the cave that is in it.” [Genesis 23:11]

But Abraham bowed again and asked to pay. So Ephron named the price, four hundred shekels of silver, and Abraham weighed out the silver in front of everyone. The field, and the cave, and all the trees in the field, became his.

And there, in the cave in the field at Machpelah, he laid Sarah to rest.

[pause]

It's a quiet detail, and easy to miss. After a lifetime of tents, of packing up and moving on, of never quite settling, the first piece of the promised land that Abraham ever owned was a resting place for Sarah. Her wandering was over.

And a few years later, when Isaac married, the Bible says he brought his bride, Rebekah, into his mother Sarah's tent, and that he loved her, and was comforted after his mother's death.

[long pause]

## What Scripture remembers

Long after, the prophet Isaiah pointed the people back to her:

> Look to Abraham your father, and to Sarah who gave you birth. [Isaiah 51:2]

And the letter to the Hebrews remembers her not for her doubts, but for her faith:

> By faith Sarah, even though she was barren and beyond the proper age, was enabled to conceive a child, because she considered Him faithful who had promised. [Hebrews 11:11]

[pause]

## Closing

Sarah was not someone with perfect faith every single day. She doubted. She grew tired of waiting. She made choices that hurt other people, and she laughed when the promise seemed too much to believe.

And God kept His word to her anyway. She is remembered, four thousand years later, as the mother of nations.

So you don't need to have everything figured out tonight. You don't need to believe perfectly, or wait without ever growing tired. The One who made the promise is faithful. That was enough for Sarah, and it is enough for tonight.

[long pause]

Let your breathing slow now.

Picture, if you like, that wide sky over the tents at Mamre. The same stars Abraham was asked to count. They are still there tonight, above the clouds and the city lights, steady and quiet, exactly where they were placed.

[pause]

Let your thoughts loosen, the way a hand loosens when it finally lets go of something it has held too tightly.

Wherever you are in your own waiting, you are seen. You are not forgotten.

Rest now.

Goodnight.

[long pause]
